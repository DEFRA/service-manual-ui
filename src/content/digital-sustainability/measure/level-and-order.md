## (i) The level of measurement: whole services or parts of services

You can measure environmental and social impacts for a whole service or for part of a service. Choose the level that supports the decision you need to make.

<div class="govuk-inset-text">

Government departments and other public sector bodies also measure environmental and social impacts for the whole organisation. This shows whether an organisation's long-term strategy is working, and makes it accountable to the public.

</div>

## (ii) The order of impact of a digital service or project

The orders of impact describe how far a service's effects reach. They range from the carbon or water its systems use to the changes in society that new technologies and services bring.

There are usually 3 orders of impact.

<div class="govuk-inset-text">

Measure first-order impacts, which your service controls. Second-order impacts need a baseline, such as the process or service being replaced, so you can find the net impact of the new service. Third-order impacts can be positive, and may be part of the policy outcomes a service aims to deliver. They can also increase environmental impacts, for example through changes in consumer behaviour that lead to more consumption.

</div>

### First-order impacts (direct effects)

These are the direct environmental and social impacts from the lifecycle of ICT hardware and digital infrastructure. They include carbon emissions, water, material use, e-waste and supply chain labour risks.

### Second-order impacts (enabling effects)

Second-order impacts are the indirect changes caused by how digital technology is applied to other sectors and processes. They can be positive (reducing environmental impacts) or negative (increasing demand).

Second-order impacts are usually described in terms of optimisation, substitution, induction and obsolescence:

### Third-order impacts

Third-order impacts are the longer-term effects of digital technologies and services on behaviour, consumption, business models and society. They include rebound effects, where efficiency gains lead to more use, which can cancel out the benefits of optimisation and substitution.

In Defra, third-order impacts may be policy objectives in their own right, such as boosting recycling rates or increasing biodiversity.
