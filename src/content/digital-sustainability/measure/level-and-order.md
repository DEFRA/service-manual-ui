## (i) The 'level' of measurement: whole services or components

Environmental and social impacts can be measured at the component or whole service level. Choose the level that supports the decision you need to make.

For example, a team could measure greenhouse gas emissions per user or transaction.</p></td></tr><tr class="govuk-table__row"><th scope="row" class="govuk-table__header">Part of a service</th><td class="govuk-table__cell"><p class="govuk-body">Helps teams understand a specific part of the service, such as data storage, software, hosting or user devices.</p><p class="govuk-body govuk-!-margin-bottom-0">Practitioners can use the results to identify improvements and assess whether their actions have been successful.</p></td></tr></tbody></table>

<div class="govuk-inset-text">

Environmental and social impacts are also frequently measured at the organisational level, for government departments and other public sector bodies. This approach can show whether an organisation's long-term environmental and social strategy is working, and demonstrates transparency and accountability to the public.

</div>

## (ii) The 'order' of impact of a digital service or project

The orders of impact for a digital service describe how its effects can range from the carbon or water used by digital systems through to the societal and systemic shifts coming from the introduction of new technologies and services.

The orders are usually divided into 3 orders of impact.

- UN Trade and Development [Digital Economy Report 2024](https://unctad.org/publication/digital-economy-report-2024) – examines the environmental impacts of digitalisation across its lifecycle, including demand for energy, water and raw materials, and opportunities to make the digital economy more environmentally sustainable.

</div>
</details>

<div class="govuk-inset-text">

Note: Measurement of the environmental and social impacts of digital services and projects should normally focus at the level of first order impacts, as these are within the control of the service. Assessing second order impacts requires measurement against a baseline (such as an existing process or service which is being replaced, in order to determine the 'net' impact of the new service) and assessing third order impacts requires assessment of policy outcomes.

</div>

### First order impacts ('Direct effects')

These are the direct environmental and social impacts from the lifecycle of ICT hardware and digital infrastructure. They represent costs to the environment and society, and include impacts such as carbon emissions, water, material use, e-waste and supply chain labour risks.

### Second order impacts ('Enabling effects')

Second order impacts are the indirect environmental and social changes caused by how digital technology is applied to other sectors and processes. These impacts can be positive (reducing negative environmental impacts) or negative (increasing demand).

Second order impacts are usually described in terms of optimisation, substitution, induction and obsolescence:

### Third order impacts

Third-order impacts are the longer-term systemic effects of digital technologies and services that result from changes in behaviour, consumption, business models and society, including rebound effects that can amplify or offset the benefits achieved through optimisation and substitution.

In Defra, these third-order impacts may be explicit policy objectives, such as boosting recycling rates or increasing biodiversity.
