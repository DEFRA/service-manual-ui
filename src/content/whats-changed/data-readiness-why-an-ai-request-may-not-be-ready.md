There is often an exciting moment at the start of an AI conversation. Someone has found a task that takes too long, information that is difficult to search, or a service that could work better.

The potential is easy to see, and the natural question is: how quickly can we build something?

One of the first things the AI Capability and Enablement team (AICE) will ask about is the data:

- What information would the capability use?
- Where is it held?
- Who looks after it?
- Is it suitable for the job, and can it be used in this way?

## AI can only work with what it is given

AI does not fix gaps or inconsistencies in data. It can reproduce them at speed and present the result fluently.

If the source material is out of date, incomplete or contradictory, the output may be too. Because the answer can still sound confident, the underlying problem may be difficult to spot.

Much of Defra's information was created to deliver a service, keep a record or meet a statutory requirement. Using it for AI is a new purpose and may need different structure, context and control.

Take a collection of guidance. An experienced colleague may know which version is current and which caveats matter.

An AI assistant will not. If several versions are available, it may give a plausible answer based on the wrong source.

## Data readiness depends on the job

Coverage matters too. Data may be accurate but still leave out particular locations, circumstances or types of case that the capability will encounter.

Before relying on it, we need to understand what the data represents, as well as what is missing.

The intended use also tells us which data is actually needed. A broad request to "use AI with our data" does not yet identify the authoritative source, the level of detail required or how current the information must be.

Defining the problem more clearly can reveal that a smaller, better-understood dataset is the right place to begin.

There is no universal test. An internal document finder may tolerate some gaps if people can check the source.

Data influencing a regulatory decision, payment or public advice needs much stronger assurance. The more significant the decision, the more important it is that the data is accurate, representative, traceable and governed.

### Data quality and suitability

Data needs to be both accurate and suitable. A dataset can be well maintained for its original purpose but have the wrong coverage, detail or update cycle for AI.

We need to understand those limitations and decide whether they are acceptable.

### Data context and meaning

A dataset rarely explains itself. Colleagues may know how it was collected, what a category means or which records should not be compared.

If this is not captured in metadata or documentation, an AI system cannot use it. Clear definitions also make answers easier to explain.

### Access, safeguards and ownership

Relevant data may be spread across systems, shared drives, spreadsheets and document libraries. Before building around it, we need to know where the authoritative source is, who owns it and how it can be accessed consistently.

Some requests involve personal data, sensitive records or information collected for another purpose. We need to know whether it can be reused, where it can be processed, who should have access and how long it should be kept.

These safeguards should shape the work from the start.

Ownership matters after launch. Someone must keep the data current, correct errors and respond to change.

A successful pilot quickly loses value if nobody owns its sources.

### Testing the data as well as the output

Before building, we should know how we will test the data and the answers it supports. We might:

- review a representative sample for gaps
- agree test questions with subject experts
- try unusual cases

Output quality should always be considered alongside the quality and coverage of the data behind it.

## "Not ready" does not mean "no"

If the data is not ready, the next step may be to:

- narrow the use case
- identify an owner
- review a sample
- resolve an access question

A controlled proof of concept can also test an assumption and show what preparation is needed.

Not every idea needs a large data transformation. A small, well-understood dataset may support a useful first step, provided the pilot exposes readiness gaps rather than hiding them.

AICE is here to help teams work out what is possible, what evidence is needed and how to move forward responsibly.

Asking about data early is not about putting a barrier in front of a good idea. It gives that idea a better chance of becoming a capability people can trust and keep using.
